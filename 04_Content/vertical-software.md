## The software these verticals already run.

Each of these started the same way. Someone who understood one industry properly
decided the software in it was not good enough, and built the version they would
actually want to use.

That company is still good at that. Knowing an industry properly is the thing
that took years, and it is the reason the business works. Fullsteam keeps that
knowledge, keeps the brand, and puts a larger company behind it.

Open any vertical to read the story.

---

## Hospitality

Hotels, restaurants, bars, and the smaller places that do a lot of different
jobs at once. A room, a table, a rental, a tab, and a kitchen that needs to know
about all of it before the dinner rush starts.

The hard part of hospitality software is that the day never repeats. A booking
that came in at midnight, a walk-in at six, a banquet setup that changed twice,
and a server who needs the table status right now. Most of the software in this
industry is built by people who love hospitality and had to learn software. The
best of it is genuinely excellent.

What a bigger company adds is the boring, expensive stuff nobody wants to build:
reliable payments that settle the way the business expects, insurance that
matches how a hotel or restaurant actually carries risk, and support that does
not disappear at seven on a Friday.

**Who it serves:** operators, owners, and the people running the floor.
**A business here:** room and table management, reservations, point of sale,
guest and member records, staff scheduling.

## Weddings & Events

This industry runs on dates. A venue has to know what is booked, when it turns
over, and who is bringing what. A planner has the same problem multiplied by
every vendor involved.

The software tends to fall behind the business. What starts as a calendar for
one venue becomes a planner's whole operation, and then it becomes the way an
office of five coordinates a season of weddings with a hundred suppliers.

The work is unusually seasonal and unusually detail-heavy. A single event has
more moving parts than a month of retail, and it happens once, so there is no
learning from repetition.

Added here: payments that handle deposits and final balances and splits without
anyone reconciling by hand the week after, plus lending against a booked season
for businesses that carry the cost of a lot of gear up front.

**Who it serves:** venues, planners, rental companies, and florists.
**A business here:** booking calendars, rental and inventory, vendor and
contract management, client records, deposits and payouts.

## Wine

Wineries, distributors, and shops, with a compliance problem attached to
essentially everything they do. Allocation, vintage, distribution agreements,
tasting-room accounts, and shipping rules that differ by state and change
regularly.

A lot of the software here was built by a small team solving one state's rules,
which is why the same vendor gets bought or rewritten several times in a career.
Operators end up with a system that works and cannot be trusted to be right.

The domain knowledge is the whole business. Somebody who knows how a release
works, how an allocation list is built, and which accounts are actually
collectible is doing something that cannot be taught from a manual.

Added here: payments and lending that understand seasonal cash flow — the year
is not evenly spread and the software should not pretend it is.

**Who it serves:** wineries, importers, distributors, and retail wine sellers.
**A business here:** allocations and vintages, compliance and reporting,
distribution, cellar and inventory, customer and account records.

## Retail

The most crowded category in the industry, and the one where the gap between
software that works and software that is right is widest.

Independent retail runs on inventory accuracy. Get the count wrong and the
store sells something it does not have, or holds something it cannot sell. Chain
retail runs on the same truth at a hundred locations at once. The software
tends to be either excellent at one store or adequate at many, and rarely both.

Most of this category is served well enough. The exceptions are the operators
who have outgrown their system and cannot find a replacement that knows their
industry.

Added here: embedded payments that settle against the actual sale, hardware that
gets to the store and works, and integrations to the accounting and logistics
tools the business already runs.

**Who it serves:** independent retailers, multi-store operators, and specialty
chains.
**A business here:** point of sale, inventory, purchasing, e-commerce,
multi-location management, customer records.

## Storage & Marina

A gate, a keypad, a unit number, and a customer who is already late. The
software in this industry is mostly billing, and mostly fine, until the
business adds a second gate.

Occupancy, access control, gate hardware, insurance certificates, and late
notices are usually separate systems that disagree with each other. A customer
who is stored at will is one of the highest-value relationships in the business
and often the least well served by the tooling.

This is a category where integration does more work than invention. Making the
existing parts talk to each other cleanly is most of the value.

Added here: payments tied to the actual unit and gate, insurance that follows
the stored goods rather than sitting in a filing cabinet, and hardware that
arrives installed.

**Who it serves:** self-storage operators, boat and RV yards, and marina
operators.
**A business here:** unit and site management, gate access and hardware,
billing, insurance certificates, customer records.

## Health & Wellness

Studios, clinics, practices, and spas, where the software is often doing
clinical-adjacent work without being a clinical system. Scheduling, records,
memberships, and billing all live together, and the boundaries between them are
not always clean.

Privacy obligations are real and the operator feels them. Software decisions
that are routine in other industries get a second look here, and the cost of
getting that wrong is not a support ticket.

The businesses that do well are the ones where the software understands the
shape of the practice, not just the shape of the calendar. Retention,
memberships, and multi-provider schedules are where the category is weakest.

Added here: payments that handle memberships, packages, and insurance billing
without breaking the record, and AI that helps with the administrative load
rather than anything that touches a clinical decision.

**Who it serves:** studio owners, clinic and practice managers, and spa and
wellness operators.
**A business here:** scheduling, memberships, records, billing and insurance
claims, staff and provider management.

## Field Services

Technicians in vans, working from a phone, in buildings where the signal is bad.
Dispatch, routing, work orders, parts, and proof the job got done — mostly by
someone whose whole job is being somewhere else at a fixed time.

The software has to work offline and be right the first time, because a
technician who has to call the office to check something is standing still and
getting paid by the hour.

The category is dominated by a handful of large platforms built for the biggest
operators, and by spreadsheets at the other end. A six-truck business is
frequently too small for the platform and too established for the spreadsheet.

Added here: payments collected in the field, lending against equipment and
receivables, and hardware that survives a van.

**Who it serves:** HVAC, plumbing, electrical, and specialty service operators.
**A business here:** dispatch and scheduling, work orders, mobile technician
access, parts and inventory, invoicing and payment collection.

## Transportation

Dispatch, load assignment, tracking, and a pile of compliance. The software
tends to assume a certain size of fleet, which is why the larger carriers run
software they have outgrown and the smaller ones run software built for a
business twice their size.

The job itself is not complicated to describe — move freight, prove where it
went, get paid — and it is unforgiving to run badly. Margins are thin enough
that an unpaid invoice or an idle truck is a real event.

The durable businesses are the ones with genuine operational depth, and that
depth is usually built on software nobody chose carefully.

Added here: payments that clear faster, lending against the book of business,
and fuel and maintenance spending that behaves.

**Who it serves:** carriers, brokers, and freight operators.
**A business here:** dispatch and load management, tracking, driver records,
compliance, invoicing and payment, fuel and maintenance.

## Automotive

Dealers, independent shops, and specialty shops, with a service department
running on a schedule and a parts department running on inventory, and a
customer who expects to be treated well in both.

The software in this industry is broad and uneven. The big dealer-management
systems assume a volume and a building size most independents do not have. The
lightweight tools do scheduling but fall apart on parts, or on the history that
makes a returning customer worth keeping.

Service history is the asset. A shop that knows what a car has had, what it
needs next, and who owns it can sell the next job without arguing. Most
software treats that as a reporting problem rather than the core of the
relationship.

Added here: payments at the counter, financing the customer can get without
leaving, and insurance that can be handled without a phone tree.

**Who it serves:** independent garages, dealer service departments, and
specialty shops.
**A business here:** work orders and scheduling, parts inventory, customer and
vehicle history, invoicing, financing and insurance.

## Association Management

Associations, societies, clubs, and trade bodies, where the business is
membership and the product is the association itself. Joining, renewing,
dues, events, committees, and a board that needs to see all of it.

This is one of the least-served categories in the industry. There are
membership-management systems built for large national associations with
sophisticated requirements, and small-club tools for clubs with a few hundred
members and no staff. In between is a large number of associations running on a
system that fits neither.

The operators are usually small, often one or two people, and deeply embedded
in a community that is also a source of new members.

Added here: dues and event payments without the reconciliation, plus AI that
handles the routine member correspondence the office gets buried under.

**Who it serves:** trade associations, professional societies, and clubs.
**A business here:** member records and renewals, dues, events and registration,
committees and directories, board reporting, member communication.

## ERP

The hardest category to generalise, because "ERP" means a different thing in
manufacturing, distribution, and services. What they share is the need to know
what is in stock, what is on order, what is owed, and what to do next — before
making a decision that costs money.

The systems in this space are the most capable and the most expensive to change.
A business rarely replaces its ERP because nothing is broken. It replaces it
because the business outgrew a decision made ten years ago, and that change
takes a year and disrupts everything.

The vendors that survive it are the ones with real industry depth. Changing an
ERP is not a software project; it is an operational one, and the reason many
stall is that the industry was not understood well enough.

Added here: payments and lending integrated with the transactional core, plus
AI that helps with planning and exception handling instead of more reporting.

**Who it serves:** manufacturers, distributors, and service operations.
**A business here:** inventory and production, purchasing, sales and order
management, financial control, reporting and planning.

## Notes for review

- **No vertical count is written in prose.** The page shows eleven tiles, and
  this copy never says a number. That deliberately sidesteps the OI-5 conflict
  (eleven verticals here vs "13+ industries" in V2, §22.6.2) — **if the client
  settles on a count, it goes in the hero or a kicker, not scattered through the
  copy.** One page, one number.
- **Embedded Offerings is named but not itemised.** Payments, lending,
  insurance, and AI are referred to functionally because they cut across all
  eleven. The dedicated explanations stay on `/offerings`, per §14.
- **Hardware appears only where it is genuinely load-bearing** — gates in
  storage, terminals and peripherals in retail, equipment in field services.
  It is not promoted as a category (KB §14 folds hardware into the offering
  pages).
- **AI is framed as administrative relief**, never headcount, and never in
  clinical or safety judgement in Health & Wellness (§20.6). That was a
  deliberate constraint.
- **No named businesses, no case studies.** Per KB §20, nothing here identifies
  a specific acquisition. Vertical descriptions are written at industry level so
  none of them reads as a portrait of a company we actually bought.
- **Excluded per §11:** no product descriptions or feature lists, no licensing,
  no employee bios.
- **Deferred:** these eleven stories are the largest single block of copy in the
  site. If the first draft needs trimming, this is where to cut depth — the
  "Who it serves" and "A business here" lines can go before the story prose.
